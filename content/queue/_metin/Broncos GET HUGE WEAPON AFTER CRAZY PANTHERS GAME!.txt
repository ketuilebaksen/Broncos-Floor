Before we talk about any transactions, we have to talk about that Kansas City win, because it sets the stage for everything else. Denver was down 11 points in the fourth quarter. Eleven. Against the Chiefs. That’s the kind of hole where a lot of teams just quietly fold, start thinking about next week, and accept the loss. Not this group.

The Broncos clawed their way back. Jonah Coleman punched in the go-ahead touchdown, and Denver walked off that field with a 28-24 victory. Guys, that’s a statement win. That’s the kind of game that changes how a locker room sees itself.

And I love the fight. I love the refusal to quit. But here’s the thing. Winning that way doesn’t make the injuries disappear. A comeback doesn’t heal a hamstring. It doesn’t put anybody back on the field who’s sitting on injured reserve. If anything, a win like that makes every decision around those injuries more important. Why? Because now Denver has something worth protecting. This isn’t a team playing out the string. This is a team with a real chance, and when you have a real chance, you can’t afford to let depth problems quietly drain it away.

And that brings us to Tuesday. Because on Tuesday, the front office made five roster changes, and every single one of them deserves a closer look.

Here’s the rundown. Denver signed defensive back Ugo Amadi, running back Jaret Patterson, and defensive tackle Jordan Miller to the practice squad. At the same time, wide receivers Eli Pancol and Cam Ross were released.

Now, let me be clear about something right away. These additions don’t automatically put anybody into Sunday’s lineup. A practice squad signing alone doesn’t tell us who’s getting called up, and it definitely doesn’t tell us who’s going to play meaningful snaps. So I’m not going to oversell any one of these names.

But here’s what really catches my attention. Look at the spread. A defensive back, a running back, and a defensive tackle. Three different position groups. That’s not random. That tells me Denver is looking at the roster and identifying the spots where injuries or limited depth could leave the coaching staff scrambling. They’re building insurance in exactly the places where they feel exposed. And honestly, that’s smart roster management.

Let’s start with Amadi, because he’s the most experienced player in this group.

Seattle drafted Ugo Amadi in the fourth round back in 2019, and since then, he’s appeared in 85 regular season games. That’s real NFL experience. Over his career, he’s put together 208 tackles, two sacks, 12 tackles for loss, two forced fumbles, and an interception. Not flashy numbers, but those are the numbers of a guy who’s been on the field, who’s been trusted, and who knows what an NFL Sunday feels like.

And here’s a detail I really like. Amadi also spent time on Denver’s practice squad back in October. So this isn’t his first introduction to the building. He’s been in the meeting rooms. He knows the terminology. He knows the coaches. When you’re trying to get somebody ready to contribute quickly, that familiarity is worth a lot. You’re not starting from zero. You’re picking up where you left off.

But let’s be honest about what matters more than those career totals. Amadi can help in the secondary, and he can help on special teams. That’s his value right now. I wouldn’t pencil him into a starting safety job. That’s not what this signing is about. What I see is an experienced option who gives the staff somewhere to turn if another player can’t go on Sunday.

And after what happened against the Chiefs, I completely understand why Denver would want that extra insurance. The safety situation has already forced players into roles they weren’t carrying earlier in the season. Guys who were mostly special teamers suddenly found themselves taking defensive snaps in a high-pressure game. That’s not a sustainable plan for the rest of the year.

That’s also why I don’t want anybody overlooking special teams when we judge these additions. A reserve player often has to prove himself there before he gets real work on defense. If a guy can cover a kick, make a clean tackle, and stay mentally ready for an emergency assignment, that helps your football team. It might not show up in the highlight package, but coaches notice. Coaches always notice. And that’s how a practice squad player turns into a roster player.

Now let’s talk about what’s actually going on at safety, because this is where the Amadi signing really makes sense.

Brandon Jones went into the Kansas City game already dealing with a hamstring injury, and then he aggravated it during that game. And that distinction matters. This wasn’t some brand new problem that popped up out of nowhere on Sunday. This is an injury that’s been lingering, and lingering hamstrings are tricky. They can nag a player for weeks if you’re not careful.

His absence opened the door for J.L. Skinner, who had mostly been contributing on special teams. And guys, Skinner made the most of it. With Kansas City threatening at the goal line late in the game, Skinner stopped Kenneth Walker for no gain. In that moment, with the game on the line, a player who had mostly been a special teams contributor stepped up and made a goal line stop.

To me, that’s exactly the kind of play that earns a reserve more trust from the coaching staff. That’s a play you can put on the screen in the film room and say, this is what preparation looks like.

And remember, Talanoa Hufanga was unavailable too. He missed his third consecutive game. So Denver was down multiple safeties against one of the best offenses in football, and that helps explain why adding another defensive back makes so much sense right now.

But I want to be careful here. The Amadi signing doesn’t tell us when Hufanga will return. And it doesn’t settle how the safeties will line up once everybody is healthy again. That’s a question for another day. For now, this is about getting through the next few weeks with enough bodies to compete.

What I loved most about Skinner’s moment was the preparation behind it. In this league, you don’t get to choose when your chance arrives. It might come in the fourth quarter, at the goal line, against the Chiefs, with the whole stadium on its feet. You either have to be ready or you don’t. Skinner was ready when Denver needed him, and that stop helped protect the lead. That’s what depth is supposed to look like.

Now let’s move to the running back room, because this is where things get really interesting, and where the bigger trade conversation starts to heat up.

Jaret Patterson gives Denver another option in the backfield. He’s played for Washington and the Chargers, including six games for Los Angeles in 2025. That season, he carried the ball 41 times for 59 yards and a touchdown. Across his NFL career, he has 139 touches for 584 scrimmage yards and three touchdowns.

Now, I’ll be straight with you. Those are modest numbers. So I’m treating this signing as a chance for Patterson to earn a role, not as Denver solving its running game. Nobody should look at this move and think the backfield problems are fixed. That’s not what this is.

But, Broncos country, I have to tell you about his college career, because his biggest college game was absolutely ridiculous. At Buffalo in 2020, Patterson carried the ball 36 times for 409 yards and eight touchdowns against Kent State. Four hundred and nine yards. Eight touchdowns. In one game. That performance tied the FBS single game record for rushing touchdowns.

You don’t accidentally put together an afternoon like that. That takes vision, that takes toughness, and that takes a motor that just doesn’t quit. Still, I’m not going to use a college performance to promise NFL production. The NFL is a completely different animal. The question now is simple. Can Patterson handle whatever assignment Denver gives him, protect the football, and contribute when the coaches call his number? That’s it. That’s the job.

Here’s why that extra option matters so much right now. R.J. Harvey is on injured reserve with a knee injury, and Tyler Badie is on injured reserve as well. Harvey’s earliest eligible return is week 13 against Miami. So the bye week isn’t going to immediately solve that shortage. Denver has to play real games before Harvey can even think about coming back.

Then there’s Cody Schrader. He was elevated for the Chiefs game, and afterward he returned to the practice squad. He’s still available as depth, but let’s be clear, that temporary elevation wasn’t a permanent promotion. It was a short-term fix for one afternoon. And Denver needs to plan beyond getting through one afternoon at a time. You can’t run a season on emergency patches.

Meanwhile, J.K. Dobbins has dealt with injuries of his own, and the ground game still hasn’t consistently made life easier for Bo Nix. And that’s the real issue. When the run game isn’t reliable, everything falls on the quarterback’s shoulders. Every third down gets longer. Every drive gets harder. Defenses can pin their ears back and attack.

But I’ll tell you what, Coleman gives me a real reason to be encouraged. His winning touchdown against Kansas City came right after a 51-yard catch and run that put Denver in position to score. Think about that. The biggest play of the drive didn’t come on a handoff. It came on a short completion that Coleman turned into something huge.

That’s a great reminder that helping your quarterback isn’t limited to taking handoffs. Getting a back into space can punish a defense just as much. When a running back can catch a short pass and turn it into 50 yards, defenses have to respect him on every single snap. Linebackers can’t cheat. Safeties can’t sit. And that opens up everything else.

To me, the next step is figuring out what Denver can actually count on every single week. A reliable running game keeps third downs manageable. It gives Nix more opportunities to work off play action. It takes pressure off the offensive line in pass protection. But building that kind of consistency takes more than just choosing a different ball carrier. The blocking has to be there. The play calls have to put that back in a position to succeed.

I want Sean Payton to build on what Coleman does well, while being honest about what the offense still isn’t doing consistently. That’s the balance. Lean into the strengths, but don’t pretend the weaknesses aren’t there.

And for me, the evaluation of any back starts with the ordinary snaps. Not the highlights. The ordinary stuff. Can he get the available yards when there isn’t a huge hole? Can he carry out his responsibility when the ball goes somewhere else, whether that’s picking up a blitz or selling a fake? Those quieter plays are what determine whether a coach feels comfortable leaving a young back on the field in big moments. The highlight plays get you noticed. The ordinary plays get you trusted.

Alright, let’s move to the defensive line, because Jordan Miller brings the focus right back up front.

Denver had Miller on its practice squad in both 2024 and 2025, and he competed with the team during the 2026 offseason. He joined Miami’s practice squad in September before finding his way back here. He’s a 6-foot-3, 307-pound tackle out of SMU, and he hasn’t appeared in a regular season NFL game yet.

So the Broncos clearly know this player. They’ve seen him in practice for multiple seasons. They know his work ethic, his strengths, and his limitations. But familiarity doesn’t mean he’s already earned meaningful snaps. He still has that work ahead of him, and nobody’s handing him anything.

Now, I’ve seen some people take this signing and try to turn it into a bigger injury story about Zach Allen. I wouldn’t do that. Allen was listed as questionable before the Chiefs game, and he was active on Sunday. Adding another defensive lineman to the practice squad doesn’t tell us that Allen’s condition has become more serious. Let’s not create a story that the facts don’t support.

What I do see is value in having somebody available who has practiced in this system before. Over the rest of the season, Denver may need help from players who aren’t getting any attention today. That’s just how the NFL works. Injuries happen, the season grinds on, and suddenly a name you barely knew is out there making plays in December. This signing gives Miller another opportunity to become one of those names.

Now, the receiver releases deserve some context too, because I know some of you are wondering what they mean.

Eli Pancol had only recently joined Denver, while Cam Ross had already spent time with the organization during the season. Their departures opened up practice squad spots as the team adjusted its depth. That’s the simple explanation.

And I wouldn’t take those cuts as proof that an injured receiver is ready to return. Practice squad decisions and an injured player’s medical clearance are two completely separate things. One doesn’t automatically tell you anything about the other.

But it does bring us to a player whose availability could make a real difference for this offense. And that’s Pat Bryant.

Bryant went on injured reserve in October after an ankle injury. Week nine is his earliest eligible return, which means a comeback is possible. Possible, not guaranteed. And I want to be careful about that. I want Bryant back when he’s truly ready to help, not just because the calendar says he’s eligible. Eligibility is a date. Readiness is a medical decision. Those aren’t the same thing, and rushing a player back from an ankle injury can backfire fast.

Now, here’s where the timeline gets really important, so stay with me.

Denver faces Carolina on November 8th, and then enters its week 10 bye. The trade deadline is Tuesday, November 10th. So that deadline decision arrives during the bye week, not after Denver has had the entire break to evaluate everything. That’s a tight window.

The Carolina game gives the staff one more look before the deadline. That’s a useful chance to see how the available players handle their roles, how the patched-up secondary holds up, and how the backfield functions without Harvey. But the front office can’t put every decision off until after that game. It has to weigh what it sees on Sunday against the options it’s already been exploring behind the scenes.

And let’s be realistic about the bye week. It can help with recovery. It can help with preparation. It’s a chance for banged-up players to rest and for coaches to self-scout. But it can’t guarantee a return date for anybody, and it definitely can’t give Denver extra time once the trade window slams shut. When that deadline passes, the roster is what it is.

Now, beyond these immediate moves, I’m also watching the longer-term decisions involving Ja’Quan McMillian, Marvin Mims Jr., Brandon Jones, and Riley Moss.

These are players Denver has every reason to value. Whether it’s McMillian’s work in the secondary, Mims’ contributions as a receiver and returner, or what Jones and Moss provide defensively, these guys matter to this team.

And I want to be clear. I’m not suggesting new contracts are about to happen. That’s not what I’m saying at all. What I’m saying is that any outside addition at the deadline has to fit alongside the players Denver may want to keep long term. Every dollar and every draft pick is connected. You can’t evaluate a trade in a vacuum. That’s a separate consideration from Tuesday’s practice squad moves, but it’s absolutely part of the bigger picture.

And that brings me to the name everybody wants to talk about. Jonathan Taylor.

Ian Rapoport and Adam Schefter reported Denver’s interest in Taylor. And when two of the most connected reporters in the league are attaching your team’s name to a player like that, it’s worth taking seriously. That’s a specific, credible reason to discuss the possibility.

But, Broncos country, let’s keep our heads here. Interest isn’t an agreement. Until a trade is officially announced, Jonathan Taylor remains a Colt. Plenty of reported interest at the deadline never turns into an actual deal.

That said, I completely understand why the idea gets people fired up. A proven runner like Taylor could change what this offense asks of its quarterback. It could give Sean Payton a completely different way to attack defenses. Suddenly, defenses have to load the box, and that opens up play action, deep shots, everything. The appeal is obvious. Anybody can see it.

Deciding what that improvement is actually worth, though, is much harder.

Personally, I’d want Denver to consider the whole deal, not just the name. There’s the draft capital going out the door. There’s the financial commitment that comes with a player at that level. And there are the opportunities that would remain, or disappear, for the younger backs already on this roster.

I’d also want a crystal clear plan for how to use Taylor, because acquiring a talented player doesn’t automatically fix every blocking issue. If the line isn’t opening holes consistently, even a great back is going to have some frustrating afternoons. Talent helps, but it doesn’t erase structural problems.

If Denver truly believes Taylor can make a meaningful difference in the games that matter most, the big games in December and January, then exploring it makes complete sense. I just wouldn’t let the excitement of a recognizable name make the price irrelevant. That’s how teams end up regretting deadline deals.

And there’s another side to this calculation that I think gets overlooked. The games Denver has to play before its injured players return. Waiting carries its own cost. If the current rotation can’t handle those weeks, the Broncos could lose ground they can never get back.

That doesn’t mean a trade is mandatory. It means the front office has to compare the immediate need with the longer view. Short term versus long term. Right now versus the future. That’s the tension every contender faces at the deadline.

And yes, Breece Hall belongs in this conversation too, as another possible option. There’s been public discussion about him as a fit for Denver. But that doesn’t establish that the Jets are ready to move him, and it definitely doesn’t mean the Broncos have a deal lined up. I’d keep that possibility in its proper place. It’s a conversation, not a transaction.

The bigger point is this. There may be several different ways to improve this backfield, and the best choice depends on availability, on cost, and on what the coaches believe they already have in Jonah Coleman. If the staff believes Coleman is ready for a bigger role, that changes the math completely.

Denver has added options in the secondary, in the backfield, and on the defensive line, all while facing immediate injury questions at multiple positions. Those transactions matter on their own. They show a front office that’s paying attention and actively protecting this team’s depth.

Whether something bigger follows is the decision still ahead. And that’s the part that has me on the edge of my seat.

I want the Broncos to stay aggressive about improving this roster, without treating every available player as the answer. Be bold, but be smart. This team earned a huge win against Kansas City.
