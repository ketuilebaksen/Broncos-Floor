With Sean Payton and this staff heading into the bye week, I think we could be looking at the beginning of something nobody saw coming.

Let’s get into it.

Look, all week long, everybody was talking about Daiyan Henley. Denver had agreed to bring him over from the Chargers, and then the whole thing fell apart after his physical. Suddenly, everyone wanted to know why the trade failed, how the two teams handled it, and why Jim Harbaugh was so upset about the whole thing.

It was drama. It was headlines. It was exactly the kind of story that eats up the entire news cycle.

But while Denver was dealing with the Henley situation, the Broncos quietly made another important move. And almost nobody noticed.

They restructured Talanoa Hufanga’s contract. They converted part of his salary into a signing bonus, and that created approximately 3.9 million dollars in cap space.

Now think about that timing. The Henley trade fell apart. But Denver still kept that additional financial flexibility.

That’s the move I’m watching. This is the kind of paperwork most fans scroll right past. It’s not exciting. It doesn’t come with a highlight reel. But here’s the question that keeps coming back to me. Why create that flexibility now? Right as the bye week gives this front office time to step back and think about the roster?

Teams don’t do things like this by accident. There’s usually a reason. And I think the reason could be bigger than most people realize.

Now, here’s where the money situation gets a little tricky, so stick with me. Cap projections depend on how bonuses and contracts are counted, not just the money available today.

According to Over the Cap, Denver has roughly 7.8 million dollars in available cap space this year. The projections show around 29.5 million for 2027, and 82.4 million for 2028.

But guys, those numbers aren’t set in stone. Not even close. Player incentives, contract extensions, and future roster decisions can all change the picture in a hurry.

And there’s more to this than just the headline numbers.

Take Evan Engram. His deal includes receiving incentives, with extra money tied to certain production marks and a playoff berth.

Nik Bonitto has contract escalators tied to sacks and defensive honors, including Defensive Player of the Year. And honestly, the way he’s been playing, you can’t rule that out.

And J.K. Dobbins has an incentive tied to reaching 1,200 yards from scrimmage, worth another 2 million dollars.

Those details matter, because a team’s final cap position changes depending on whether players hit those marks or not. The number you see in November isn’t always the number you end up with in March.

And here’s the other side of the Hufanga restructure. It helps the current year, absolutely. But it also pushes some of that accounting into later years. And don’t forget, there’s a significant cap charge sitting in a 2028 void year if his deal isn’t changed again.

So let me be clear. I don’t see this as free money. Nothing in the NFL is free money. I see it as Denver choosing flexibility at a very interesting moment.

So the big question is, who benefits?

There are four names I’d put on the board right now. Ja’Quan McMillian. Marvin Mims Jr. Riley Moss. And Brandon Jones.

All four have done something important for this team. All four have made plays that mattered. And all four raise different questions about what Denver should pay to keep them.

Maybe one of them gives Denver a hometown discount because he loves it here. Maybe another wants something closer to market value. And then there’s the kind of money a player can demand when he knows other teams might be lining up for him in free agency. Every one of these situations is different.

But there’s another piece to this puzzle too.

When John Franklin-Myers left for Tennessee, Denver put itself in position for a projected compensatory draft pick. So if another player leaves in free agency, compensation could become part of the calculation. It’s never automatic, and the league’s formula is complicated, but it’s a factor.

And here’s one more wrinkle. If Vance Joseph were hired as a head coach somewhere else, the league has a separate process that could bring Denver two third round picks, as long as its requirements are met. Now, nobody wants to lose Vance Joseph. But it’s part of the bigger picture when you think about how this front office plans for the future.

Now, of those four players, I keep coming back to Ja’Quan McMillian first.

Think about his story. He came into this league undrafted out of East Carolina. Nobody handed him anything. Nobody spent a high pick on him. And he worked his way into being one of the most important pieces of this entire defense.

He can get into the backfield. He can pressure quarterbacks. He can break up passes. And he can take the ball away.

Remember that 44-yard pick six against Jameis Winston back in December 2024? Or how about his overtime interception against Sam Darnold this year? That pick set up Wil Lutz’s game-winning field goal against Seattle. Overtime. Game on the line. And McMillian made the play.

Those are moments that change games. And that’s what I love about him. A quarterback has to worry about his coverage and the possibility that he’s coming after him on a blitz. He makes you think twice. He makes you hesitate. And in the NFL, hesitation kills drives.

And let’s talk about what he’s actually doing out there, because playing nickel corner is no easy assignment. On the outside, a defender can use the sideline as help. It’s like having an extra defender. But inside, in the slot, receivers can break either way. There’s traffic coming from every direction. Running backs, tight ends, crossing routes. It’s chaos in there.

That’s why I pay so much attention to what McMillian does. He brings timing, he brings toughness, and he brings the confidence to attack the football when a play is on the line.

And he’s getting noticed inside the locker room too. Pat Surtain II has made it clear that McMillian deserves a serious payday. When the best cornerback in football is publicly saying you deserve to get paid, that means something. His teammates know exactly what he means to this defense. And I think the front office needs to take that seriously.

So what would the price look like?

Kyler Gordon got a deal with Chicago worth roughly 13 million dollars a year. If McMillian wants to push beyond that kind of number, a hypothetical 14 million dollar annual deal is a reasonable place to start the conversation.

Now, let me be clear. That’s not an announced offer. That’s not a report. It’s just a way to understand the decision Denver is facing.

And here’s my take. I don’t want the Broncos waiting until somebody else’s offer makes the decision for them. Because that’s what happens when you wait. Another team comes in with a big number, and suddenly you’re either overpaying or watching him walk. If this is the player you want, why wait for the price to climb?

But I wouldn’t stop with the defense, because there’s another player I would hate to lose, for a completely different reason.

That’s Marvin Mims Jr.

He can flip field position in a matter of seconds. One return and suddenly your offense is starting in great position. But I’ve always felt his value goes way beyond bringing kicks back.

He’s willing to do the unglamorous work. The stuff that doesn’t make highlight reels. And then he’ll turn around and make a big play on offense when the team needs him. That kind of player gives a coach options every single week. And for me, that’s exactly the kind of useful, flexible player a winning team should try to keep.

And think back to the AFC Championship Game against New England. Troy Franklin was unavailable because of his hamstring injury. Pat Bryant was active. And even with Jarrett Stidham starting at quarterback, Marvin Mims Jr. stepped up when Denver needed him most.

He made a huge 52-yard catch against Christian Gonzalez, one of the best corners in the league, and helped put the Broncos in position to score. In the AFC Championship Game. With a backup quarterback.

That’s exactly the kind of play that shows why keeping him matters. When things get uncomfortable, when the pressure is at its highest, he doesn’t disappear. He shows up.

Sean Payton has talked again and again about finding ways to get him the football. And the more Bo Nix builds trust with a player who can help in so many different roles, the more valuable that connection becomes.

If Denver can reach a fair, middle-of-the-market deal with Mims, keeping that chemistry would be a smart investment, without forcing the team into an unreasonable commitment.

Now, choosing those two would make the next decision a lot harder. And that brings us to Riley Moss.

I think a lot of people underrate how difficult his job has been. Think about it. When Pat Surtain takes away a team’s biggest weapon on one side of the field, where do quarterbacks start looking? The other side. Right at Riley Moss.

He’s been tested over and over again. And when Surtain has been unavailable, Moss has had to carry even more responsibility. He’s battled through those assignments, and he’s shown why this defense trusts him.

But here’s where the money gets complicated. Let’s say Riley Moss would agree to something around 17 million dollars a year. Depending on what the market looks like, maybe that’s even a team-friendly number. But that’s still a serious commitment. And another team might value him even higher.

Now, Denver does have other options. Kris Abrams-Draine has handled different assignments for this defense. And former first round pick Jahdae Barron gives the staff another option to work with.

I’m not saying either one is guaranteed to replace Riley Moss perfectly. That’s not my point. My point is that depth changes how much risk you can take with a contract. If you have young players developing behind him, you have more leverage at the negotiating table.

And if Moss walks and signs a big deal somewhere else, a compensatory pick might eventually follow, depending on the league’s formula. That’s worth considering. But let’s be honest, a draft pick wouldn’t erase the loss of a good starting corner.

And I don’t want us to overlook Brandon Jones either, because this decision deserves more attention than it’s getting.

He’s already come up with two interceptions this season. He plays a lot of important snaps. He communicates with the guys around him, and he brings a steady voice to the secondary.

When you pair that with what Talanoa Hufanga gives this defense, you understand why keeping those two together is so appealing. Good safety play isn’t always flashy. A lot of it happens before the ball is even snapped. Getting everyone lined up. Making the right check. Seeing the formation and adjusting. Jones does that.

Still, Denver has to look at its backups. J.L. Skinner has received positive attention from Vance Joseph for stepping in when his number was called, including that huge goal line stop against Kansas City. And the team also drafted Miles Scott out of Illinois. He’s a developmental option, not someone we can already call the answer at safety. But those are players the staff needs to evaluate over the rest of this season.

So for me, Brandon Jones remains someone worth trying to keep. Just not ahead of the two players who offer the most unique value.

And this is where that quiet Hufanga restructure really has me thinking.

Because when you put all the pieces together, a picture starts to form. Denver created cap space at a moment when nobody was paying attention. The Henley trade fell through, which means that space wasn’t used. And the bye week gives the front office time to sit down, look at the roster, and plan.

So what if the plan is bigger than one trade? What if Denver is setting itself up to lock in its own players before they ever reach the market?

Now, let me be clear again. Nothing has been announced. I’m not reporting a deal. This is me connecting the dots. But think about it with me.

What if Denver tries to extend both Ja’Quan McMillian and Marvin Mims Jr.? Two homegrown players. Two guys who have made huge plays in big moments. Two guys who fit exactly what Sean Payton and Vance Joseph want to do.

Getting McMillian done early could keep his price from climbing if he keeps making plays down the stretch. Every interception, every sack, every big moment pushes his number up. Lock him in now, and you might save real money.

And getting Mims done could keep a weapon in Bo Nix’s hands for years to come. Their chemistry is growing. Why let that walk out the door?

And here’s where the void year situation and the future cap projections come in. With around 82 million dollars projected for 2028, Denver has room to structure deals in a smart way. Push some money down the road, keep the current year flexible, and keep this core together while Bo Nix is still on a manageable deal.

That’s the window, Broncos country. That’s the window every smart franchise tries to take advantage of. When your quarterback is young and affordable, that’s when you lock in the players around him. That’s when you build the team that wins it all.

And if Denver can do that, it doesn’t have to make a choice between Moss and Jones right now. It can keep its options open, let the season play out, see who steps up, and make those decisions when the time is right.

That’s the beauty of flexibility. It doesn’t force you into anything. It gives you choices.

So when I look at this quiet little restructure, I don’t just see paperwork. I see a front office that’s thinking two steps ahead. While everybody was focused on the trade that didn’t happen, Denver may have been setting up the moves that really matter.

And honestly, that’s what great organizations do. They don’t chase every headline. They make the quiet moves that set them up for long-term success.
